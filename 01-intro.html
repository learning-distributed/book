Distributed Systems: Principles and Practice

Introduction

“When experts are wrong it's often because they are experts on an earlier version of the world.” - Paul Graham

What is a distributed system?

A distributed system is a set of physically separate computers working together to provide some useful computation. The computers working together may be geographically dispersed, spread across different countries or even continents, or physically nearby, such as adjacent servers in the same data center. Both are distributed systems as we discuss them in this book.

What is the difference between a distributed application and a distributed service?

Two closely related concepts are distributed applications (such as Google Maps and many others) and distributed services (such as Amazon Cloud or Microsoft Azure). A distributed application is a user-facing distributed system, often split between some code running on the user's device and other code running on the application provider's computers. A distributed service is a distributed system used by other distributed systems to get their work done.

Using multiple computers, either nearby or geographically remote, raises several difficulties:

  • Each computer operates independently of the other computers participating in the computation.
How can data be stale?
  • Computers in a distributed system typically do not have direct access to each other's memory. Since communication takes time, any information one computer receives about another's state could be stale, or out of date, by the time it receives and acts on it. Resource sharing is also harder when application state needs to be moved between machines, rather than simply using different cores on the same machine.
What is a partial failure?

  • One or more computers may crash during the computation, while others remain operational. These failures can be due to software or hardware faults, or power failures. Indeed, the larger the distributed system and the longer the computation lasts, the more likely such a partial failure will occur. Many distributed systems are so large that dealing with partial failures is the common case.

  • Network communication between computers is itself implemented by a distributed system, subject to its own complex partial failure modes.

Reasoning About Distributed Systems

How can we produce useful and correct computations despite these issues? A key step is to carefully reason about the possible system states and transitions. To provide some intuition for that reasoning, we give two simplified examples, the first due to Joseph Halpern and the second popularized by Jim Gray.

Muddy Foreheads

Suppose we have a classroom of young children. The children are, as most children are of a certain age, smart, attentive, and well-meaning. To avoid distractions, their teacher collects their smartphones at the beginning of the day and has removed any mirrors from the classroom.

After playing outside during recess after a rainstorm, some of the children have some mud on their foreheads. Before washing up, the children are instructed to come in quietly and sit in a circle,
illustrated below

where Alice and Bob happen to be the ones with mud on their foreheads. The teacher says everyone please look at everyone else around the circle and then close your eyes. Once everyone's eyes are closed, the teacher says, without speaking or communicating with each other, please raise your hand if you are certain you have mud on your own forehead. Having given everyone a chance to raise their hand, the teacher says keeping your hand raised if it is raised, now everyone open your eyes.

Quite obviously, no child raises their hand. How could they, if they can't see their own forehead? Further, if the teacher repeats the task - get everyone to look around again at everyone else, close their eyes, raise their hands if they are sure they have mud on their forehead, and then open their eyes - still no one will raise their hand. Indeed, it doesn't matter how stubborn the teacher is. No matter how many times they repeat the test, no one will raise their hand.

Suppose now the teacher changes the game slightly. The teacher says out loud to everyone, some of you have mud on your forehead. Now let's repeat the experiment. Can a child raise their hand now? Yes, in the special case where only one child has mud on their forehead. If a child sees that no one else has mud on their forehead, the teacher has given them useful information and so they can answer correctly.

What if more than one child has mud on their forehead? Then the teacher's statement that someone has mud on their forehead appears to add no new information to what the children already know - they can all see that someone else has mud on their forehead.

Nevertheless, if the teacher repeats the task enough times, eventually every child with mud on their forehead will raise their hands, and indeed, they will all do so at the same time. How is that possible if the teacher hasn't told the children anything new? Remember, the children are all smart, attentive, and well-meaning. If there are only two children with mud on their forehead, then two of the children will see that someone else has mud on their forehead, but during the first round they didn't immediately raise their hand, then the only possible explanation is that they themselves must have mud on their forehead. If there are three children with muddy foreheads, no one will raise their hand during the first two rounds, but all three will raise their hands in the third round, using similar logic. We can extend this to any number of muddy foreheads by induction on the number of rounds.

While the teacher didn't say anything that everyone didn't already know, the teacher did add something crucial - knowledge that every other child knows that some children have muddy foreheads. One can imagine a slightly different scenario where the teacher whispered to each student in turn that there was someone with a muddy forehead. With more than one child with a muddy forehead, we would be stuck - no one would raise their hand, no matter how many times we try, because there is no basis to reason from the actions of the other children.

There is a difference between knowledge (I can directly see some other child has a muddy forehead) and knowledge of what others know. The teacher told everyone that some child has a muddy forehead, and so now every child knows everyone knows that some child has a muddy forehead. This lets each child reason on that basis that they must have a muddy forehead, if indeed that is true.

This difference between reasoning about what one computer knows, and what that computer knows about what other computers know, is central to the study of distributed systems.

Two Generals Paradox

Almost all networks in practice sometimes fail to deliver some of their messages. At a physical level, communication between omputers over distance is encoded as a signal over a noisy channel. WiFi, cellular communication, Ethernet, and fiber optic links all have noise, and they use various error correction systems to detect and discard corrupted data packets. Even with error detection and correction, the amount of noise fundamentally limits the maximum rate that we can send information over the channel. Put another way, when networks are designed to send as much data as fast as possible, as most are, we must expect some likelihood of packet loss.

The potential for data loss can make it difficult to coordinate the participants in a distributed system. To illustrate this, consider two generals, encamped on two separate hills, who need to coordinate an attack on the opposing army in the valley. This is illustrated below

with generals Alice and Bob. (Sadly the smart, attentive, and well-meaning children in the previous example have grown up to be militaristic.) If they both attack at the same time, the attack will succeed, and otherwise it will fail. Further suppose that this is in the age before cellphones, it is a foggy day so they can't use signal flares, and the distance between the hills is too far for sound to travel reliably. The only way for Alice and Bob to communicate is to send encrypted messages by foot through the enemy controlled region. Some of the messengers may be captured, but possibly some of them will get through.

Assuming Alice and Bob had not previously agreed on a time to attack, can we devise a way for them to agree on a course of action? Suppose we start by having Alice send Bob a message, suggesting a time for the attack. Suppose the message gets through to the other side. This is shown below

Can they now both attack? Obviously not, since Alice does not know if Bob received the message. The message might not have arrived.

We can try to fix this by having Bob send a message back to Alice in reply. Suppose that message gets through as well, shown below.

Can they now both attack? At this point, Alice knows that Bob got the original message, but Bob does not know if its reply message was received. If Alice won't attack unless Alice receives the reply, then Bob can't be sure that Alice will attack, e.g., if the reply message was lost.

We can try to fix this by having Alice send a reply message in response to Bob's reply. Suppose that message gets through.

Can they now both attack? We leave to the reader to convince themselves that no amount of back and forth can guarantee that both sides have the same knowledge. This is true even if, as in the example, all messages are in fact delivered. The mere possibility that the messages might not reach the target makes it impossible to achieve joint agreement, unless the two parties already had agreed on the outcome before they started.

These two examples show is that common knowledge - what everyone knows everyone knows - is a powerful tool for building systems, but in a system where participants use a lossy communication network to learn about each other's state, common knowledge is only possible about past, and not current, states of the system.

Why Distributed Systems?

If reasoning about distributed systems is more difficult than reasoning about sequential systems, we could ask: why do we need distributed computers in the first place? After all, writing useful and correct programs is, if not easy then at least easier, if the program runs on only one computer rather than when it is spread over many computers. If programming distributed computers is hard, maybe we just shouldn't do it.

One answer is that the concepts described in this book enable programmers using distributed systems concepts to operate at a simpler, higher level of abstraction, even if that makes the task of the programmers developing those abstractions more difficult. There are usually more users of a distributed system than builders of the system, so this tradeoff is often worthwhile. For example, one of abstractions we discuss in this book, linearizability, is complex to implement in the presence of geographic distribution, caching, and server or network failures. Once implemented, however, linearizability provides a conceptually simple model for programmers using the abstraction to develop distributed applications.

Just a few decades ago, almost all software was "shrink wrap" - printed on physical media and shipped to the end user to install and run on a single machine. That is not true anymore. Almost all modern software - both applications and systems - are distributed systems:

  • Group collaboration systems, e.g., Github and Google Docs.
  • Video streaming systems, e.g., TikTok or YouTube.
  • Group messaging applications, e.g., WhatsApp and Signal.
  • Online marketplaces, e.g., Amazon and Etsy.
  • Advertising platforms, e.g., Google Ad Manager.
  • Enterprise customer management, e.g., Salesforce.
  • Travel booking sites, e.g., Booking.com and Expedia.
  • Global navigation software, e.g., Google Maps and Uber.

Even solitaire games - the most standalone thing one could imagine - now often have a part of their functionality that is distributed, if they are advertising supported, maintain user statistics across multiple devices, or update software automatically.

There are several reasons why almost all modern software is distributed:

  • People are physically distributed, but want to be connected, so the applications that they use to communicate and share information also need to be distributed. Email got its start as a way for users with accounts on the same machine to share information. Of course, that is limited:
    what if you want to send an email to someone at another school or in a different location? Email became one of the earliest distributed systems because you need computers to work together to provide a global email system. Of course, today we have many different ways for people to use computers to stay connected, beyond email; many of these, such as instant messaging, also got their start as a way to communicate among users on a single system,
    before making the leap to being distributed.

    What is the network effect?
    The benefit of distributed systems to each user often increases with the number of users. This is called the network effect, and was first observed for telephones. A telephone is more valuable the more people who have telephones. The same can be said about email, social networking, and other information sharing applications. By extending the reach of information sharing applications to everyone on the planet, distributed applications can become much more valuable to their users than their single machine counterparts.

What is a data center?

  • Information starts out distributed, but needs to be merged and redistributed for it to have the most value. A data center is a place for the centralized computer processing and serving of data. Data processing often follows the network effect, in that the more data that is analyzed together, the more valuable the result.

    Initially, data centers were single computers - often whatever was the largest and fastest computer of the time. They became distributed systems as a way to cost-efficiently build even larger and faster systems to keep up with the exponential increase in the data to be aggregated and analyzed, first unleashed by standardized web protocols for publishing and retrieving data on a worldwide scale, but continuing today as distributed applications themselves collect data to improve their operations.

    By collecting data in one place, we make it easier to extract useful information. For example, the choice of which pages to present in response to a search query is based on the graph structure of the interrelationships between web pages; Google's PageRank algorithm successively approximates the importance of each page based on the importance of the pages with links to that page. More recently, rapid advances in machine learning and artificial intelligence are enabled by training models on the widest possible variety of data. Collecting that data in one place makes machine learning analyses more efficient.

    Many useful applications are only economically feasible if their development and operational costs can be prorated over the largest possible set of users, that is, at global scale. For example, it reportedly costs Google Maps more than a billion dollars each year to run, manage, and update its maps. Of course it is not just maps: many useful applications assemble and organize huge streams of distributed information; process the data at a scale that far exceeds what any single computer could handle; and serve their data to large numbers of users spread around the planet.

  • Data isn't only collected, it also gets redistributed. Most content - news articles, photos, YouTube or streaming videos, restaurant reviews - has a one to many distribution pattern, with each source consumed by many users. Indeed, application software updates, such as to repair a potential security vulnerability, are automatically distributed to every device, rather than needing to be shipped on physical media and installed by the user.

    What is a content distribution network and where is it?
    For many applications, the time to fetch data from a centralized data center - e.g., the place where it was created or stored - adds noticeably to user response time. Serving data centrally also adds considerably to the cost of distribution, as content needed by many users is downloaded by each user in turn over long distances. This has led to the creation of a special type of distributed system called a content distribution network that aims to place and serve copies of data near to the users that want that data, to reduce latency and transmission costs. Some companies like Google and Meta are reported to maintain servers - called points of presence - in thousands of locations around the globe to ensure that content is almost always nearby the users who want to access that content. In turn, we need distributed systems concepts to keep those copies up to date when the underlying data changes.

  • Computers can fail, but users want systems that are always up. Leslie Lamport once quipped that a distributed system is one where you can't get your work done because some machine you have never heard of has failed. If a machine that has the only copy of some data that you need, and it crashes, there is little to do but wait until it comes back online. By spreading data across many systems, distributed systems can make this problem much worse. If your work depends on a large number of computer hardware and software systems all functioning correctly at the same time, the user-visible uptime - how frequently the system is usable - will be no better (and often a lot worse) than the uptime of its flakiest component.

    Today, user-visible failures of the most popular applications are so rare that it can make the news if a widely used application is offline even for an hour.
    What has changed? Although distributed systems have many components that can fail, we can use distributed systems concepts to mask failures so that users don't notice them. When one computer fails, another can step in to do its job. With enough spares, we can continue to provide service almost without regard for how many simultaneous failures there are. The distributed systems protocols to mask failures discussed in this book are among the most complex algorithms in all of computer science, but they are also among the most commercially important.

How frequently do servers fail?

Because distributed systems can mask failures, it can be difficult to obtain accurate data on the types and frequency of failures affecting distributed systems.
However, in 2009, Jeff Dean published data on this, at the cluster level within Google's data centers. At the time, a cluster was a group of several tens of thousands of servers, connected together with a high speed network and with its own dedicated disk storage. Each cluster was large enough to do substantial data processing tasks on its own, such as to compute Google's search index or to serve a substantial number of gmail clients. A large data center might have several such clusters. This organization is still used today. The numbers reported are no longer current, as different distributed systems companies have become better over time at reducing the number of these types of errors; however, all of these types of errors still do occur in practice.

For a newly installed cluster, over the course of its first year, Dean reported that Google experienced failures of all sorts:

  • One half (that is, a 50% chance) of a cluster-wide overheating event that required powering down most machines in the cluster within a few minutes to avoid damaging the hardware, and took 1-2 days to bring the cluster back to full operation. Collecting servers in one place is more efficient, but it also makes cooling a challenge, as a data center can generate a vast amount of heat. If the cooling system fails, servers must be powered off in order to avoid permanent damage.

  • One failure of a power distribution unit (PDU) affecting 500-1000 machines. Power must be distributed from the utility down to each server, and this distribution network can fail. Recovery from a PDU failure can take six hours.

  • One rack move affecting 500-1000 machines. From time to time the machines in a data center might need to be moved from one part of the data center to another. This is an example of planned maintenance where the distributed applications running on those systems are given sufficient warning of the planned change, to allow them to migrate work onto other servers unaffected by the outage.

  • One network rewiring, affecting a rolling 5% of the machines over a multi-day span. The networks connecting servers can also need to be upgraded, and generally that disrupts those servers from communicating during the disruption. This is another example of a planned maintenance operation.

  • Twenty rack failures. A rack is a set of 40-80 servers housed in a standardized column. Servers within a rack may individually fail, but sometimes the entire rack can become disconnected if the network switch fails for that rack fails. Sometimes the disconnection is partial - the nodes within the rack may be ablet to communicate with each other, but not to the rest of the cluster or data center.

  • Five rack networks go "wonky." A considerable problem in data centers are gray failures. A gray failure is when a node or network continues to operate, but at far below its normal operating parameters. An example is when the network switch suddenly starts dropping half of the packets to or from every server in the rack; some data gets through but only with vastly lower throughput.

  • Four network reconfiguration operations that go awry and cause substantial, temporary packet loss for a period of a half hour.

  • Three router failures that disconnect the data center from the outside world for an hour.

  • Twelve router reloads that also disrupt external connectivity, but only for a few minutes.

  • 1000 individual server machine failures. As we mentioned, we can use replication to hide these failures. Generally, the replacement can be any server in the rack (if the failure is limited to one server), or to a server in a different rack (if the network switch fails), in a different cluster (if the cluster power distribution unit fails), or in a different data center (if the data center power or cooling fails, or connectivity to that data center is lost).

  • Thousands of hard drive failures. Disk storage is also a distributed system that needs to be organized to be resilient to failures. Disk storage can also experience gray failures, where the disk is partially functional but at much lower througput.

There are also a number of documented cases of rarer failures, such as DRAM sometimes returning data different from what was originally stored there, CPUs that sometimes produce unexpected results when executing certain types of rare instructions, wide area network cables that are damaged by someone digging where they weren't expected to, and so forth. In other words, anything that can go wrong, probably will at some point. Distributed systems need to plan for and gracefully handle all of these types of failures, in order to mask them from end users.

  • Computers are finite, but demand for computing is elastic. The first commercial web search engines were built using the fastest multicore servers of the time, with a copy of the data replicated on several computers to spread load in case one of them failed. Today, a single search query can involve lookups spread over hundreds or even thousands of computers, because the computation and storage needed far outstrips the capacity of even the largest servers. When the most popular applications have billions of users, it isn't feasible to implement them without relying on distributed systems.

    What is incremental scalability?
    Application load is not static. Developers are continually adding new features that require additional computation and storage, attracting more users who in turn require even more resources. Distributed systems provide a way for systems to have incremental scalability - the ability to add capacity to a computer system in small increments on an as needed basis. Indeed, most applications see demand that ebbs and flows across the day. There is about a factor of three difference between the minimum and maximum number of people who are awake at any point during the day. So we need systems that not only can scale up with added demand, but also scale down and turn off their computing resources as demand wanes in order to save energy.

  • Computers are expensive, but distributed systems can reduce those costs. It is often cheaper and simpler to build large scale systems out of many simpler small computers than it is to build a few very large scale systems of the same capacity. The same goes for storage - it is more expensive to build one very large disk than many smaller disks with the same aggregate capacity. There is a rule of thumb in computer manufacturing that every factor of two increase in volume decreases the cost of manufacturing by 10%. One reason is that engineering development costs can be spread over a larger number of units; another is that it is easier to learn how to improve systems when you have a lot of examples to learn from. Companies spend more half a trillion dollars per year on data center servers, storage, and networks, with about 15 million servers manufactured each year. Replacing those with a much smaller number of more powerful machines is perhaps possible, but it would lead to much higher costs.

    As we mentioned above, there is also a need to be able to incrementally increase resources as an application adds users and features. By building systems out of a large number of small components, we can provision systems with just the resources they need at the moment, rather than needing to purchase a single, much larger system in advance of potential future growth, leaving much of its capacity wasted until it is needed. This also reduces costs.

  • Shared memory is convenient, but it increases the blast radius for software errors. There is a tradeoff, of course. It is often easier to program a single, shared-memory computer than it is to coordinate the various parts of a distributed system, especially when some of those parts can fail or communicate over a network that can also have complex failure modes. With shared memory, work can be easily shifted between cores, increasing resource efficiency when demand for any single application is variable.

    However, when there is a software failure, such as an illegal pointer dereference, how do we know what has been affected? The true cause of the error might have occurred much earlier, and only detected after several other data structures have been corrupted. When an application fails, the operating system generally wipes the application's memory on that machine and restarts it from a prior checkpoint. What if the operating system itself has a memory error? The operating system kernel has the ability to overwrite any part of any application's memory and disk storage; it needs that power in order to be able to set up and manage application memory. In most data centers, any operating system failure requires resetting all of the applications on that server. Because distributed systems do not share memory, other copies of the application running elsewhere can continue to service users while the failing copy is repaired.

    It is not impossible to imagine that we could build very large systems with the ability to isolate application and operating system failures to an individual core so that just that core is affected by a failure; that are constructed out of small, replaceable components to manage costs; and that have the ability to incrementally expand as needs or workloads change. Such a system would need an internal architecture very similar to the distributed systems we describe in this book.

    Even if we could somehow run all of Google or Meta's applications on a few giant shared-memory computers, that would mean that any failure would cause a noticeable drop in the capacity of the system to serve users. For the most popular applications, it is common to spread the work across many data centers, so that even losing an entire data center (a rare, but not unpredictable event) will not cause user-visible application slowdowns. Indeed, Amazon is reported to intentionally (and randomly) disconnect one of the data centers supporting its e-commerce site on a regular basis, as a way of testing whether the error recovery in that case really works. It would be disastrous for Amazon for a data center to fail during Black Friday and only then discover that the error recovery code was flawed.

  • Mobile devices are power limited, but computation and state can be offloaded. While distributed applications have become more complex, the devices we use to access those applications have become smaller, lighter, and power constrained. Most popular applications have responded by splitting their functionality between a "frontend" that runs on the user device and a shared "backend" that runs on centralized servers and provides common functionality for all users of that application.
    Indeed, the modern smartphone would be much less useful without a way for applications to call on services beyond what fits on the device. Because users of any one application may be geographically distributed, the backends of many applications are implemented as a distributed system running across a set of data centers, shared among the users of that application.

    Another common feature for many smartphone applications is that they maintain context when you switch devices - if your smartphone needs to be recharged, you can use a tablet instead. This means that a copy of application state must be stored off the device, e.g. in a data center, not solely in the file system of the specific device. When switching devices, the user sees an up to date view of the same state. A more general case is provided by collaboration software like Google Mail or Google Docs, where users can edit in disconnected mode, with data re-synchronized through the data center once connectivity is restored. Any device with access to the underlying data sees the same view. This also needs distributed systems concepts to ensure that the re-synchronization occurs correctly and without anomalies from the user's point of view.

    What is REST?
    A common paradigm for mobile applications is called REST for REpresentational State Transfer. With REST, the application frontend running on the user's device is intentionally kept simple and focused on user-interface issues, while the shared backend stores application state and implements the bulk of the application logic. As the user makes state changes, the frontend pushes those changes to the backend (the representational state transfer). If the network connection drops (e.g., when a device goes out of WiFi range, it must reconnect over cellular), the device can reconnect and re-sync state without the user noticing. The frontend caches data to hide the extra latency of communicating with the backend, while the backend uses distributed systems concepts for implementing the application logic in a fault tolerant manner.

    While REST may seem a step backward - why not use the computational capacity of the end user's device if it is available - it offers benefits to end users, application developers, and the companies that provide those applications, to a degree that more than makes up for the limitations of the REST programming model.

    What is cloud computing?
    Google and Apple reportedly have more two million applications for download onto a smartphone. Where do the backends for all these applications run? Cloud computing allows companies (and users) to rent computing resources; an early use case was (and is) to host the backends of mobile and web applications. By multiplexing many applications on the same physical infrastructure, this saves costs: resources unused by one application can be easily redirected to another, and applications can request just the resources they need at the moment instead of each application needing to buy and maintain extra computers for its own anticipated future demand. Cloud computing also centralizes system administration, further lowering costs.

  • Distributed programming is hard, but distributed systems enable accelerated application development. In a famous paper in 1986, Fred Brooks Jr. claimed that there was no "silver bullet" that would be able to improve the productivity of software developers by an order of magnitude. Yet software today is almost incomparably more capable and useful than it was forty years ago. The economy employs about ten times more software developers today than it did when Brooks wrote his paper, but the improvement in software quality and reach is far more than what can be explained by that factor alone. It may be hard for anyone who didn't live through it to grasp, but sending an email cross-country in 1986 required the user to identify (by hand!) a sequence of intermediate servers that would get the email closer to the destination. Computers used to crash and lose data on a nearly daily basis.

    What accounts for this improvement in software quality, even as almost all software has shifted to a distributed systems model where we need careful reasoning to understand whether systems work? Distributed systems can aid software development in ways that more than make up for any additional complexity.
    Brooks identified three concrete ways to improve software productivity: software reuse, rapid prototyping, and growing software organically by adding features only after they are being used.

    Distributed systems massively aid software development in all three dimensions. (Brooks also mentions a fourth - hire better software developers; the astronomical stock valuations of companies building distributed systems makes it easier for them to afford the best software engineers.)

    First, the move to host application logic in the cloud has led to extensive software reuse. In the shrink wrap era, reusing a software library meant linking it in the same process as the main program. This required resolving any conflicts in how each library uses shared resources like the heap. It also requires ensuring the library works in all hardware and operating system configurations where the application needs to run. Further, any software errors such as a misused pointer or missing error check in one piece of code affects the reliability of the entire application. The larger the codebase linked together, the more likely that a pointer error will cause the entire application to fail. Quality assurance was challenging in the shrink wrap era for both the library developer and the application developer.

    What is Software as a Service (SaaS)?
    A different model is to build and sell all types of software as a service (SaaS). In the SaaS model, both applications and the libraries they depend on are hosted as standalone services that use network communication to make requests of one another. This makes software reuse much easier, as each service is responsible for its own operating environment, including
    how many resources to use, what other libraries to call, how to charge for the service, and so forth. Errors are limited to each service by design, with both the caller and the callee programmed defensively to ensure that mistakes in one service don't cause downtime in another.

    This approach enables code reuse on a massive scale. Meta, for example, reports that their applications rely on more than forty thousand shared services hosted inside Meta's data centers.

    Rapid prototyping is also easier with modern distributed systems.
    The existence of extensive libraries of shared code allows new applications to be started with minimal effort. There is no longer a need to integrate and ship physical software, as in the shrink wrap era. Indeed, this book was written as a rapid protyping effort, with chapters shared with students as they were being written.

    Perhaps the greatest win is the ability to grow software organically, learning from experience in order to understand which features are most important. Software as a Service systems enable continuous integration and deployment, where software is developed, tested, and deployed to users in a matter of days. Users can provide feedback by interacting with a working prototype, rather than a description of the concept of the feature, with lessons applied quickly. By contrast, in the shrink wrap era, software releases took months or even years, requiring a top down design with careful planning and staging of features.
