Distributed Systems: Principles and Practice

Preface

Overview

Welcome to this book!

Distributed Systems: Principles and Practice is an online textbook for a senior undergraduate or masters course in distributed systems. It is also appropriate for students studying the material on their own.

Many of our students say that the material in this book - based on a course we have taught for the past decade at the University of Washington - is among the hardest, most interesting, and most relevant courses that those students took during their time as undergraduates.

Part of the excitement in learning distributed systems, and for us in teaching the material, is that the topic combines two otherwise separate threads of academic computer science education: systems and theory. On one side, computer science systems classes focus on practical considerations - how do we build systems to be useful. On the other, computer science theory classes provide formal rigor - how can we be sure that algorithms do what we think they do?

What is perhaps unique about distributed systems is that formal rigor is key to building systems that are useful in practice. All useful distributed systems are both concurrent and fault tolerant. Concurrency arises from the independent operation of the computers comprising the distributed system; once we have multiple computers working together, partial failures - failures of some components but not others - becomes an inherent feature of system operation. What does it mean to compute a result correctly despite the failure of some (but not all) of a system's components?

Both concurrency and fault tolerance require careful reasoning. For sequential programs, we can tell whether a program works correctly on some input by running the program. Does it produce the expected output? For a distributed system, however, that isn't nearly good enough. Perhaps one part of the distributed system runs a bit faster the next time through, or the failure occurs on a different computer and/or at a slightly different time. How do we convince ourselves that the design works in all cases?

This is the key intellectual challenge for students learning this material, and helping students to overcome that challenge is our goal in writing this book. One of the most important learning outcomes is to think like a distributed systems developer - how do we reason about the behavior of an inherently concurrent system of physically separate computers, without shared state between those computers, and where some of those computers can fail, while the system as a whole continues to provide correct answers to users?

Course Project

We strongly encourage instructors and students to pair this book with some course implementation project. We have experience teaching difficult distributed systems concepts to thousands of students over the course of the past decade. In our view, asking students to implement key concepts, and then rigorously validating those implementations, is crucial towards deepening student understanding of the material.

While we have written the book to be independent of any specific course project, one candidate to consider is dslabs (link), an open source project we developed for the distributed systems class we teach at the University of Washington (UW). dslabs is notable because it embeds a formal model checker that automatically validates students code against literally millions of possible event interleavings and failure scenarios. While that might seem extreme, we believe engaging students in the struggle to make systems work under all failure conditions is key to getting students to notice and repair where their conceptual understanding is fuzzy or incomplete. dslabs has been adopted at many schools, both in the US and internationally, but it is not the only option. For example, faculty at MIT have developed a different open source project that teaches many of the same topics.

The dslabs project walks students through the construction of a linearizable, fault tolerant, highly available, dynamically sharded, transactional key-value store, through a series of four assignments. If those words seem like jargon, do not despair - this book explains the meaning of "linearizable", "fault tolerant", "highly available", "dynamically sharded", and "transactional", including how these concepts are implemented in practice. The resulting system, built from scratch by students except for some framework code needed by the model checker, is similar to what underpins resilient state management in the most widely used planetary-scale distributed applications, such as search, global mapping software, AI model serving, e-commerce, content distribution, and social networks.
At UW, students complete the dslabs project within a high tempo ten week quarter, but dslabs is also appropriate for (and frequently used by) schools on a semester system.

Pre-requisites and Related Courses

Although we teach distributed systems primarily to seniors, distributed systems can be taken fairly early in a computer science curriculum since it has relatively few pre-requisites.
As perhaps an extreme example, a student completed our class while still in high school (hi Alex!). For this textbook, we assume knowledge of data structures and a basic familiarity with mathematical reasoning. If paired with the dslabs project, students should also have some experience debugging complex code.
Prior exposure to reasoning about concurrency is helpful but not required.

This textbook can be placed within the broader agenda of teaching students how to build correct, efficient, and secure computer systems. Several other systems courses address closely related topics. For example, at UW, we offer these courses as complements to our distributed systems course:

  • Operating Systems - this course focuses on the management and scheduling of the hardware resources of a single multiprocessor machine on behalf of multiple users. A key challenge in operating systems is how to isolate potentially buggy or untrustworthy software from affecting the correctness of the operating system and the other application programs running on it. Another is to schedule resources efficiently when demand exceeds supply. Concurrency and fault tolerance are essential topics in both operating systems and distributed systems - modern computers are almost always multiprocessors, and they sometimes crash, whether due to a power outage, hardware fault, or software error in the operating system itself. For example, we want to construct file systems so that they do not become corrupted or lose saved user data regardless of whether or when the machine crashes. Many of the same principles that apply to distributed systems also apply in the construction of file systems.

  • Computer Networks - this course concerns how to provide secure, reliable, and efficient communication between computers on a planetary scale. Computer networks can be seen as a specialized kind of distributed system for the purpose of communication. Fault tolerant algorithms are crucial, as we need the network to continue to work even when individual network links, switches, or routers fail. One of the primary goals of the Internet was to provide this property. Another similarity is that network protocols need to interoperate between different machines and implementations written by different engineers and companies. That means our smartphones don't only talk to servers run by Apple or Google or Samsung. One important difference is that distributed systems often need to manage application state in a consistent manner across partial failures, while networks generally assume communication is stateless (from the network's perspective) and can be safely restarted.

  • Database Systems - this course (at UW a two course sequence on database abstractions and their implementation) concerns ways to efficiently manage and compute against large amounts of data. A key underlying abstraction in database systems is the transaction - the atomic and durable update to concurrent state. Transactions are also a key abstraction in distributed systems. UW's database classes focus on the implementation of the transaction abstraction for individual machines, while this textbook (and our distributed systems class) explains how the idea can be extended to work across networks of servers.

These courses, along with distributed systems, are not sequenced; students are allowed to take them in any order. Students interested in a full understanding of how computer systems work are encouraged to take as many of these courses as possible.

UW also offers several cross-cutting systems classes that integrate concepts and approaches from multiple perspectives, including distributed systems:

  • Concurrency and Parallelism - this recently introduced course is designed to expose students earlier in the curriculum to the concepts of shared-memory concurrency and the formal reasoning about the correctness of concurrent programs. If such a class exists at your school, it would make some of the concepts in this textbook a bit easier to understand. However, we do not assume prior experience with concurrency as a pre-requisite.

  • Datacenter Systems - datacenter and cloud systems have become the primary way to do large scale computing. The topic relies on and integrates the perspectives of operating systems, networking, databases, and distributed systems, while going into more depth on the specific challenges of datacenters, such as virtualization, distributed resource management, capacity planning, and queueing theory.

  • Mobile Systems - at the other end of the spectrum, edge computing offers its own set of challenges while relying on many of the concepts drawn from these same courses. Because smartphones and other mobile devices are close to the user, this class offers a way to understand systems concepts as they apply directly to user interaction with computers.

In other words, there is a lot to learn! We ourselves have taught a majority of the above courses; to be honest, we find teaching distributed systems to be the most fun.

Topic Outline

The outline of this textbook follows the sequence we use to teach the material at UW. We plan to release chapters as they are written, and so we note below which chapters are available now versus planned for the near future. Because student patience is limited when covering abstract or theoretical material without a grounding in practice, we interleave more theoretical and foundational chapters with those that are more pragmatic and immediately useful. This interleaving also enables students to start on their implementation project earlier in the term; we strongly recommend covering the material in such a way as to give students more time to struggle with the hardest concepts. Of course, instructors are encouraged to mix and match the ordering of topics as they see fit.

  • Chapter 1: Introduction. (Under construction) This chapter provides students an initial exposure to the challenges posed by distributed systems; outlines goals and evaluation metrics; and discusses internal architectural design decisions underpinning many large-scale distributed systems.

  • Chapter 2: Foundations. Before we discuss how to build distributed systems to achieve certain goals, we need to formalize what we can assume, and what shouldn't assume, about the behavior of the underlying computing and communication infrastructure. For example, we need to define the ways in which faults manifest so that we can tell whether our algorithms will succeed or fail when they meet failures in the real world.

  • Chapter 3: Remote Procedure Call. Remote procedure calls (RPCs) are an extremely widely used abstraction, used billions of times per second by cloud and smartphone applications worldwide.
    Using a key-value store as an example, we describe how RPCs are used and also how they are implemented. This introduces students to the basics of failure analysis against a defined fault model, in a setting where it is simple to reason about correctness.

  • Chapter 4: Virtual and Physical Time. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 5: Checkpointing and Debugging. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 6: Primary Backup. (Under construction) Primary-backup protocols are the workhorse of fault tolerant distributed systems. Using a key-value store as a working example, we show how to build a system that can tolerate (some) server faults in addition to (some) network faults such as those addressed in the previous chapter. Our focus in this chapter is to expand the range and complexity of considerations students consider when reasoning about correctness, as well as to present concrete protocols that are used in practice.

  • Chapter 7: Memory Semantics. (Under construction) Linearizability, strict consistency, stale reads, eventual consistency, memory fences, and other concepts.

  • Chapter 8: Distributed Objects.

  • Chapter 9: Consensus. The impossibility of the same. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 10: Paxos and MultiPaxos. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 11: Leases. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 12: Sharding and Load Balancing. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 13: Fault Tolerant Cache Coherence. (Under construction) For linearizable and weaker consistency models.

  • Chapter 14: Distributed Transactions. (Under construction) Two phase commit for multi-key operations on sharded storage, and its highly available implementations.

  • Chapter 15: Disconnected Operation and Eventual Consistency. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 16: Planetary Scale Replication. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 17: Microservices. (Under construction)

  • Chapter 18: Security. (Under construction)
